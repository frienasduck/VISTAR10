MCQPYQ 2019
Which metal is stored under kerosene to prevent it from reacting with air and water?
A. Gold
B. Copper
C. Sodium
D. Magnesium
▼
✅ Answer: C — Sodium
Sodium reacts vigorously with O₂ (forming Na₂O) and with water (NaOH + H₂↑, may catch fire). Kerosene is an inert liquid that prevents contact with air and moisture. K and Li are also stored in kerosene.
Board Tip: Na, K, Li → kerosene. White phosphorus (non-metal) → stored in water (catches fire in air). Both storage rules come up as 1-mark questions.

The most ductile metal is:
A. Iron
B. Gold
C. Copper
D. Aluminium

Gold is both the most ductile and most malleable metal. 1 g of gold can be drawn into a ~2 km wire. It can be beaten into gold leaf less than 0.0001 mm thick.

MCQPYQ 2022
Which metal does NOT react with dilute hydrochloric acid?
A. Zinc
B. Iron
C. Copper
D. Magnesium
▼
✅ Answer: C — Copper
Copper lies below hydrogen in the reactivity series and cannot displace H₂ from dilute acids. Zn, Fe, and Mg are all above H and react with HCl to give H₂ gas.
Board Tip: Metals BELOW H (Cu, Ag, Au, Pt) do NOT react with dilute acids. This is one of the most tested single facts in Chapter 3.

MCQPYQ 2018
Thermite, used to weld railway tracks, is a mixture of:
A. Fe₂O₃ and Al powder
B. Fe and Al₂O₃
C. Fe and Cu powder
D. CuO and Al powder
▼
✅ Answer: A — Fe₂O₃ and Al powder
Thermite reaction: Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe + heat (~2500°C). Al (more reactive) displaces Fe, producing molten iron that fills the gap between rail ends. It is an aluminothermic displacement reaction.
Board Tip: Thermite reaction = displacement + redox + exothermic. Al is oxidised (→Al₂O₃), Fe³⁺ is reduced (→Fe). Used in thermite welding of railway tracks. All three descriptions may be tested.

Galvanisation protects iron by coating it with:
A. Tin
B. Copper
C. Zinc
D. Chromium
▼
✅ Answer: C — Zinc
Zinc (more reactive than iron) acts as a sacrificial anode — it corrodes preferentially, protecting iron even if the coating is scratched. Used for roofing sheets, buckets, pipes.

MCQPYQ 2021
An ionic compound in solid state does not conduct electricity because:
A. It has no charged particles
B. Its ions are held in fixed positions and cannot move
C. It decomposes when current passes
D. It is insoluble in water
▼
✅ Answer: B
In solid ionic compounds, ions are locked in a crystal lattice by strong electrostatic forces. They cannot move, so they cannot carry electric current. In molten state or solution, ions become free to move and conduct electricity.
Board Tip: Ionic solid → no conduction (ions fixed). Ionic molten/solution → conducts (ions free to move). The key word is FREE MOVEMENT. Asked as both MCQ and 2-mark explanation.

In electrolytic refining of copper, what is used as the anode?
A. Impure copper
B. Pure copper
C. Carbon rod
D. Platinum wire
▼
✅ Answer: A — Impure copper
Anode = impure copper (dissolves → Cu²⁺ ions). Cathode = pure copper (pure Cu deposits on it). Electrolyte = CuSO₄ solution. Impurities including Au, Ag, Pt settle as anode mud below the anode.

MCQPYQ 2013
The main reducing agent in the blast furnace is:
A. Coke (C)
B. Carbon monoxide (CO)
C. Limestone (CaCO₃)
D. Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
▼
✅ Answer: B — Carbon monoxide (CO)
Coke burns → CO₂. CO₂ + C → 2CO. It is CO that reduces Fe₂O₃: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Limestone is the flux, not a reducing agent. CO is the primary reducing agent.
Board Tip: Classic trick MCQ: many write "Carbon (C)" but CO is correct. C first becomes CO₂, then CO. It is CO that does the actual reduction. This is tested almost every year.

MCQPYQ 2022
Cryolite is added during electrolysis of aluminium to:
A. Act as a reducing agent
B. Lower the melting point of alumina
C. Remove impurities from bauxite
D. Increase the melting point of alumina
▼
✅ Answer: B
Pure Al₂O₃ melts at ~2000°C — impractical for electrolysis. Cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) lowers the melting point to ~950°C and improves electrical conductivity of the melt. Fluorspar (CaF₂) is also added for the same purpose.
Board Tip: Cryolite = Na₃AlF₆. Reduces Al₂O₃ m.p. 2000°C → 950°C. Carbon anodes burn away (O₂ at anode reacts with C → CO₂) and must be replaced. Al deposits at cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al.

Calcium reacts with cold water to form a milky solution because:

MCQPYQ 2023
Why does aluminium not corrode easily despite being reactive?
A. It is below hydrogen in the reactivity series
B. It does not react with any substance
C. It forms a thin impervious Al₂O₃ layer on its surface
D. It is stored in kerosene
▼
✅ Answer: C
Al is reactive but forms a hard, impervious Al₂O₃ layer (passivation) that sticks firmly to its surface, preventing further corrosion. In contrast, iron's rust (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O) is porous and flaky — it keeps falling off, exposing fresh iron.
Board Tip: Al₂O₃ = hard + impervious = protects Al. Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (rust) = porous + flaky = does NOT protect Fe. This Al vs Fe corrosion contrast is asked in HOTS-type questions every few years.

MCQPYQ 2024
A copper wire placed in AgNO₃ solution turns silvery grey and the solution turns blue. Which is the correct explanation?
A. Cu displaces Ag; Cu²⁺ ions make solution blue
B. Ag displaces Cu from solution
C. CuO forms on the wire
D. AgNO₃ oxidises the copper
▼
✅ Answer: A
Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag. Copper (more reactive than silver) displaces Ag from AgNO₃ solution. Silver deposits on the wire (silvery). Cu²⁺ ions (blue) form in solution. Colourless AgNO₃ → blue Cu(NO₃)₂.
Board Tip: Wire turns silvery (Ag deposits). Solution turns blue (Cu²⁺ = blue). Compare: Fe nail in CuSO₄ → nail turns brown (Cu), blue solution fades (FeSO₄ = pale green). Both experiments are board favourites.

MCQPYQ 2024
Which of the following metals can be extracted by carbon reduction?
A. Sodium
B. Aluminium
C. Iron
D. Potassium
▼
✅ Answer: C — Iron
Carbon can only reduce metals that are LESS reactive than carbon in the reactivity series. Iron (Fe) is below Al and above Cu — it can be reduced by coke in the blast furnace. Na, K, and Al are MORE reactive than C → they must be extracted by electrolysis.
Board Tip: C reduces: Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, Cu (metals below C in reactivity series). C cannot reduce: K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al (metals above C). Reactivity position determines extraction method.

▼
✅ Answer

What is flux? Give one example used in the blast furnace.
▼
✅ Answer

VSAPYQ 2022
How is an ionic bond formed? Give one example.
▼
✅ Answer
An ionic bond is formed by complete transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal. The metal forms a cation (+) and the non-metal forms an anion (−). The electrostatic attraction between them is the ionic bond. Example: Na → Na⁺ + e⁻; Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻; Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl.
Board Tip: Ionic bond = electron TRANSFER (not sharing). Covalent bond = electron SHARING. Metals lose electrons → cations. Non-metals gain electrons → anions. Metal + non-metal = ionic compound.

Distinguish between pig iron and wrought iron.
▼

VSAPYQ 2023
What is electrolytic refining? Name the metal most commonly refined this way.
▼
✅ Answer
Electrolytic refining is the process of purifying crude metals using electrolysis. Impure metal = anode (dissolves), pure metal = cathode (grows), metal salt solution = electrolyte. Most commonly used for copper (Cu), using CuSO₄ solution.

Give two properties of ionic compounds.
▼
✅ Answer

SA I2 MarksPYQ 2017
Why can sodium not be extracted by carbon reduction? How is it extracted?
▼
✅ Answer
Sodium is more reactive than carbon, so C cannot displace Na. Carbon can only reduce metals LESS reactive than itself. Sodium is extracted by electrolysis of molten NaCl (Downs process):
2NaCl(l) → 2Na + Cl₂ (electrolysis, molten)
Board Tip: Metals more reactive than C → electrolysis. Metals less reactive than C → carbon reduction. Molten NaCl (not aqueous) must be used because in aqueous NaCl, H₂ is produced at cathode instead of Na.

Why is zinc preferred over tin for galvanising iron?

SA I2 MarksPYQ 2019
Why do metals conduct electricity? Explain in terms of metallic bonding.
▼
✅ Answer

SA II3 MarksPYQ 2018
Why do ionic compounds have high melting points and dissolve in water but not in kerosene?
▼
✅ Answer
High melting points: Ionic compounds consist of a lattice of oppositely charged ions held together by very strong electrostatic forces. Breaking these forces requires a large amount of energy — hence high melting points. Example: NaCl melts at 801°C.

Dissolve in water (not kerosene): Water is a polar molecule. It can surround and stabilise both cations and anions (hydration), overcoming the electrostatic forces and breaking the ionic lattice. "Like dissolves like" — polar solvent dissolves ionic compounds; non-polar solvent does not.
Board Tip: "Like dissolves like" = polar solvents dissolve ionic/polar compounds. Non-polar solvents dissolve non-polar substances. Ionic compounds in non-polar solvents = no dissolution. This is a 2-3 mark conceptual favourite.

Explain the reactivity series in detail. How does it help predict: (i) displacement reactions, (ii) extraction methods, (iii) reaction with water, (iv) reaction with dilute acids?
▼
✅ Answer
Reactivity Series: Arrangement of metals (+ H) in decreasing order of chemical reactivity: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > (H) > Cu > Ag > Au > Pt.

(i) Displacement reactions: A metal higher in the series displaces a metal lower in the series from its salt solution. Example: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu (Zn above Cu). Cu cannot displace Zn because Cu is below Zn.

(ii) Extraction methods: Top metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) — more reactive than C → electrolysis only. Middle metals (Zn, Fe, Pb, Sn) — less reactive than C → carbon/CO reduction. Bottom metals (Cu, Ag, Au) — occur free or thermal decomposition.

(iii) Reaction with water: K, Na, Ca — cold water (vigorous). Mg — hot water/steam. Al, Zn, Fe — steam only. Cu, Ag, Au — no reaction with any water form.

(iv) Reaction with dilute acids: Metals above H (Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb) displace H₂ from dilute acids. Metals below H (Cu, Ag, Au) — no reaction with dilute acids.

LA5 MarksPYQ 2023
HOTS: Explain why: (a) Al is above Fe in reactivity but is used in aircraft while Fe/steel is used in buildings, (b) Na reacts with water but Au does not, (c) ionic solids are brittle but metals are malleable, (d) carbon cannot extract Na but can extract Fe, (e) galvanised iron is better than tin-plated iron for long-term outdoor use.
▼
✅ Answer
(a) Al in aircraft, Fe in buildings: Although Al is more reactive than Fe, Al is protected by impervious Al₂O₃ passivation layer — it doesn't corrode in practice. Al is also much lighter (density 2.7 g/cc vs Fe 7.8 g/cc). Aircraft need lightweight materials. Buildings need structural strength — steel (stronger) is used despite its weight.

(b) Na reacts with water, Au does not: Na is at the top of the reactivity series — it has a strong tendency to lose electrons and form Na⁺. Au is at the bottom — it is extremely stable in its elemental form and has virtually no tendency to react. Reactivity determines whether a metal interacts with environmental substances.

(c) Ionic solids brittle, metals malleable: In ionic solids, layers of ions are arranged so that same-charge ions come face-to-face when displaced — strong repulsion causes the lattice to shatter. In metals, layers of atoms can slide over each other without disrupting the "sea of electrons" — no repulsion occurs, allowing deformation without fracture.

(d) C extracts Fe, not Na: A reducing agent can only displace metals less reactive than itself. Carbon is between Al and Zn in the reactivity series — it is less reactive than Na but more reactive than Fe. So C displaces Fe (from Fe₂O₃), but cannot displace Na (Na more reactive than C).

(e) Galvanised iron better than tin-plated: Zn is more reactive than Fe → sacrificial anode effect. Even when coating is scratched, Zn corrodes and protects Fe. Sn is less reactive than Fe → once scratched, Fe (more reactive than Sn) corrodes faster (accelerated galvanic corrosion). Galvanisation provides active protection; tin plating provides only passive protection.

(iv) When two metals are mixed to form an alloy, the mixture of different-sized atoms disrupts the regular packing more than either pure metal — this lowers the melting point (eutectic effect). Solder (Pb + Sn, 50:50) melts at ~183°C, lower than pure Pb (327°C) or pure Sn (232°C).
Board Tip: German Silver (no silver = Cu+Zn+Ni) is a guaranteed mark if asked. Solder's low m.p. explains why it is used for soldering circuits (low temperature prevents heat damage to components). The eutectic concept (lowest m.p. mixture) is HOTS knowledge.

Case Based4 MarksPYQ 2018
A factory produces steel pipes and coats some with zinc (galvanised) and some with tin (tin-plated). After two years of outdoor use, the galvanised pipes remained in good condition while the tin-plated pipes showed significant rusting where the coating had scratched. The factory engineer explained that the type of metal used for coating matters enormously, not just the coating itself.
(i) Why did the galvanised pipes not rust even when scratched? [1] (ii) Why did the tin-plated pipes rust faster at the scratches? [1] (iii) Write the reaction equation for rusting of iron. [1] (iv) Name two other methods the factory could use to prevent corrosion of iron pipes. [1]
▼
✅ Answers
(i) Zinc is more reactive than iron. When the Zn coating is scratched, Zn still corrodes preferentially (sacrificial protection/galvanic protection). Zinc electrons flow to iron, preventing Fe from being oxidised. This electrochemical protection works even at scratches.
(ii) Tin is LESS reactive than iron. When Sn coating is scratched, iron (more reactive than Sn) becomes the anode in a galvanic cell with tin. Iron corrodes FASTER than it would without any coating — the scratch accelerates rusting (galvanic corrosion). Sn only provides physical, not electrochemical protection.
(iii) 4Fe + 3O₂ + xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O
(iv) Two other methods: (1) Electroplating with chromium or nickel — hard, corrosion-resistant coating. (2) Cathodic protection — attach magnesium blocks to the pipe (Mg corrodes instead of Fe). OR: Painting/coating with epoxy paint.
Board Tip: The galvanised vs tin-plated comparison is one of the most important applied concepts in this chapter. The critical insight: Zn > Fe → Zn corrodes first (good). Sn < Fe → Fe corrodes first (bad). Reactivity difference determines which coating actually protects.

Case Based4 MarksPYQ 2016
During electrolytic refining of copper, a student set up the following apparatus: Two copper rods in CuSO₄ solution connected to a battery. After 1 hour, she noticed that one rod (connected to the positive terminal) had become thinner and a brown deposit had formed on the other rod. She also observed a small amount of grey sludge at the bottom of the container, below the thinning rod.
(i) Identify the anode and cathode. What happened at each? [1] (ii) Write the electrode reactions (half equations). [1] (iii) What is the grey sludge and what might it contain? [1] (iv) If the impure copper anode contained traces of silver and platinum, where would these end up? [1]
▼
✅ Answers
(i) Anode = the rod connected to positive terminal (it became thinner = dissolved). Cathode = the rod connected to negative terminal (brown copper deposited on it = grew thicker). Anode dissolves (oxidation); cathode grows (reduction).
(ii) Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation — copper dissolves)
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (reduction — pure copper deposits)
(iii) The grey sludge is the anode mud. It contains insoluble impurities from the impure copper that did not dissolve into the electrolyte when the anode was oxidised.
(iv) Silver and platinum (being less reactive / having higher reduction potential) would not dissolve into the CuSO₄ solution from the anode. They would remain as part of the anode mud (grey sludge) at the bottom. These precious metals can be recovered and sold, making electrolytic refining commercially valuable.
Board Tip: The observation "one rod became thinner" = anode (dissolves). "Brown deposit on other rod" = cathode (pure Cu grows). Positive terminal of battery connected to = anode. Negative terminal = cathode. This practical-based question tests lab understanding.

Case Based4 MarksPYQ 2014
Megha did an experiment where she tested whether certain solids conduct electricity by connecting them in a simple circuit with a bulb and battery. She tested: solid NaCl (table salt), NaCl dissolved in water, molten NaCl, solid sugar, and copper wire. The bulb glowed only for aqueous NaCl solution, molten NaCl, and copper wire. She was puzzled why solid NaCl — which she knew contained ions — did not conduct.
(i) Why does solid NaCl not conduct electricity despite having ions? [1] (ii) Why does NaCl solution conduct but solid NaCl does not? [1] (iii) Why does molten NaCl conduct? [1] (iv) Name the type of bonding in NaCl and in sugar. Why does sugar not conduct even when dissolved? [1]
▼
✅ Answers
(i) In solid NaCl, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are held in fixed positions in a crystal lattice by strong electrostatic forces. They cannot move, so they cannot carry electric current. Conductivity requires mobile charged particles.
(ii) When NaCl dissolves in water, the ionic lattice breaks down. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions become free to move in the solution. These mobile ions carry electric current — hence NaCl solution conducts.
(iii) Molten NaCl is heated above its melting point (801°C). The ionic lattice breaks down as ions gain sufficient energy to overcome electrostatic forces. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are now free to move in the molten state → they conduct electricity.
(iv) NaCl = ionic bond (electron transfer, Na⁺ and Cl⁻). Sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) = covalent bond (electron sharing, non-metal atoms). Sugar dissolves in water but forms neutral sugar molecules (not ions) in solution. Without charged particles, there is no electrical conductivity.
Board Tip: The ionic vs covalent conductivity comparison: Ionic compounds conduct in molten/solution (free ions). Covalent compounds don't conduct at all (no ions). Sugar dissolving in water does NOT produce ions → no conduction. This is a very common conceptual question.

Case Based4 MarksPYQ 2013
During a railway repair, workers used a mixture of iron oxide and aluminium powder (thermite) packed into a clay mould between two broken rail ends. When ignited, a vigorous reaction occurred producing an intense white flame and molten iron that filled the gap between the rails, welding them together. The workers were careful to stand back as temperatures reached 2500°C. After cooling, a shiny, solid iron weld was visible.
(i) Write the balanced equation for the thermite reaction. [1] (ii) What type of reaction is the thermite reaction? Give two classifications. [1] (iii) Why is aluminium used specifically in thermite, and not carbon or another metal? [1] (iv) The reaction produces Al₂O₃ as a by-product. State one use of Al₂O₃. [1]
▼
✅ Answers
(i) Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe + heat (~2500°C)
(ii) The thermite reaction is: (1) A displacement reaction — Al displaces Fe from Fe₂O₃. (2) A redox reaction — Al is oxidised (Al → Al₂O₃), Fe³⁺ is reduced (Fe³⁺ → Fe). It is also called an aluminothermic reaction.
(iii) Aluminium is used because: (a) Al is more reactive than Fe (Al is above Fe in the reactivity series), so Al can displace Fe from Fe₂O₃. (b) The reaction is highly exothermic (produces ~2500°C) — sufficient to melt iron for welding. Carbon would not produce enough heat, and less reactive metals cannot displace Fe.
(iv) Al₂O₃ uses: (1) Used as a refractory material in furnaces (very high melting point ~2072°C). (2) Used as an abrasive (emery = Al₂O₃). (3) Protective oxide layer on aluminium surfaces. (4) Used in the Hall-Héroult process as the ore of aluminium.
Board Tip: Thermite reaction = aluminothermic + displacement + redox. ALL three descriptions are correct and tested. Al must be more reactive than the target metal for aluminothermic reduction to work. Used commercially in thermite welding (rail tracks), not just in labs.
