Section 1
Physical Properties of Metals & Non-metals
Most metals are hard, shiny, and good conductors — but there are important exceptions every board paper tests.
🔨
Malleability
Metals can be beaten into thin sheets without breaking. Non-metals are brittle and shatter.
Most malleable: Gold (Au). Gold foil = 0.0001 mm thin.
🪡
Ductility
Metals can be drawn into thin wires. 1g of gold = 2 km wire. Non-metals break.
Most ductile: Gold. Used in: copper wiring, aluminium cables.
✨
Lustre
Metals have a shiny surface. Non-metals are generally dull.
Exception: Iodine (non-metal) has metallic lustre. Graphite also shines.
⚡
Conductivity
Metals conduct heat and electricity due to free electrons. Non-metals generally don't.
Exception: Graphite (non-metal) conducts electricity. Best conductor: Silver (Ag).
🔔
Sonority
Metals produce a ringing sound when struck — they are sonorous. Non-metals are not.
Used in: school bells, musical instruments.
🌡️
Melting & Boiling Points
Most metals have high melting points. Exceptions: Mercury (liquid at room temp), Gallium (melts in hand).
Highest m.p.: Tungsten (W) at 3422°C. Used in bulb filaments.
📋
Critical Exceptions — Always Tested!
Liquid metals: Mercury (Hg) — only liquid metal at room temp.
Liquid non-metal: Bromine (Br₂) — only liquid non-metal at room temp.
Best conductor of electricity: Silver (Ag) → Copper (Cu) → Gold (Au).
Non-metal conductor: Graphite (form of carbon) — used in electrodes.
Lustrous non-metal: Iodine (I₂) — has metallic lustre.
Hardest natural substance: Diamond (form of carbon, non-metal).

Section 2
Chemical Properties of Metals
Metals react differently with oxygen, water, and acids — the pattern of reactivity is the key to understanding this entire chapter.

🔥 2A — Reaction with Oxygen

Most metals react with oxygen to form metal oxides (basic). Non-metals react with oxygen to form non-metal oxides (acidic).
Metal + Oxygen → Metal Oxide (basic)
2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (white powder, burns with brilliant white light)
4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O (sodium oxide — very reactive)
2Cu + O₂ →heat 2CuO (black copper oxide)
4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃ (forms protective layer — passivation)
Non-metal + Oxygen → Non-metal Oxide (acidic)
C + O₂ → CO₂ (acidic gas)
S + O₂ → SO₂ (acidic, causes acid rain)
💡
Amphoteric Oxides
Al₂O₃ and ZnO react with BOTH acids and bases. Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O (acts like acid). Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O (acts like base). Board Tip: Amphoteric = reacts with both. Gold (Au) and Platinum (Pt) do not react with oxygen even on heating.

💧 2B — Reaction with Water

Reactivity with water follows the same order as the reactivity series. Most reactive metals react explosively; least reactive metals don't react at all.
MetalConditionReactionProducts
K (Potassium)Cold waterViolent, catches fireKOH + H₂↑ (yellow flame from K vapour)
Na (Sodium)Cold waterVigorous, may catch fireNaOH + H₂↑
Ca (Calcium)Cold waterVigorous, no fireCa(OH)₂ (milky) + H₂↑
Mg (Magnesium)Hot water / steamSlow with hot water; reacts with steamMgO + H₂↑
Al, Zn, FeSteam onlyModerate; need high temperatureMetal oxide + H₂↑
Cu, Ag, AuNo reactionNo reaction even with steam—
2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑ (vigorous, may catch fire)
Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂↑ (milky solution, no fire)
3Fe + 4H₂O →steam Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂↑ (steam only)

🧪 2C — Reaction with Dilute Acids

Metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series displace H₂ from dilute acids. Metals below hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Au) do NOT react with dilute acids.
Metal + Dilute Acid → Salt + Hydrogen ↑
Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂↑
Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂↑
⚠️
Copper Does NOT React with Dilute Acids
Cu is below H in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace H₂. However, Cu does dissolve in concentrated HNO₃ (oxidising acid). This is a common board trap — beware of options saying Cu + HCl → reaction.

🔄 2D — Displacement Reactions

A more reactive metal can displace a less reactive metal from its salt solution in water. This is a key application of the reactivity series.
Fe + CuSO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + Cu↓ (iron nail turns copper-coloured, blue solution fades)
Zn + CuSO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + Cu↓
Cu + AgNO₃(aq) → Cu(NO₃)₂(aq) + 2Ag↓ (copper wire turns silvery)
💡
Observation-Based Board Questions
Iron nail in CuSO₄: nail turns brown/copper-coloured; blue solution turns pale green (FeSO₄).
Copper wire in AgNO₃: wire turns silvery; colourless solution turns blue (Cu(NO₃)₂).
These observations are commonly asked in 3-mark experiment questions.

🧠 Concept Check — Reactivity Series
Q1: Which metal displaces iron from iron(II) sulphate solution?
Zinc or Magnesium (any metal more reactive than iron in the reactivity series). Zn + FeSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Fe. Copper CANNOT displace iron because Cu is less reactive than Fe.
Q2: A metal X does not react with cold water but reacts vigorously with steam. Where does X lie in the reactivity series?
X lies between Ca (reacts with cold water) and Cu (does not react with steam). It is likely Mg, Al, Zn, or Fe — metals in the middle of the series that react with steam only.
Q3: Why is gold found in free (native) state in nature?
Gold is at the bottom of the reactivity series — it is extremely unreactive. It does not combine with oxygen, water, or dilute acids, so it remains in the uncombined (elemental/native) state in nature.
Section 4
Ionic Compounds & Bonding
Metals and non-metals react by transferring electrons — creating ionic compounds with unique properties.
When a metal reacts with a non-metal, the metal loses electrons to form a positively charged cation, and the non-metal gains electrons to form a negatively charged anion. The electrostatic attraction between cation and anion is the ionic bond.

Example: Formation of NaCl (Sodium Chloride)

Na (2,8,1) → Na⁺ (2,8) + e⁻ (loses 1 electron → stable octet)
Cl (2,8,7) + e⁻ → Cl⁻ (2,8,8) (gains 1 electron → stable octet)
Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl (ionic bond by electrostatic attraction)
Similarly: MgO forms when Mg (2,8,2) loses 2e⁻ → Mg²⁺ and O (2,6) gains 2e⁻ → O²⁻.

Properties of Ionic Compounds

PropertyIonic CompoundsReason
Physical stateSolid at room temperatureStrong electrostatic forces between ions in lattice
Melting/Boiling pointHighLarge energy needed to break ionic lattice
SolubilityGenerally soluble in waterPolar water molecules surround and separate ions
Electrical conductivity (solid)Do NOT conductIons are fixed in lattice, not free to move
Electrical conductivity (molten/solution)DO conductIons are free to move and carry charge
Solubility in organic solventsGenerally insolubleNon-polar solvents cannot break ionic lattice
📋
Most Common Board Question on Ionic Compounds
"Ionic compounds do not conduct electricity in solid state but conduct in molten state or in aqueous solution."
Reason: In solid state, ions are held in fixed positions in the crystal lattice. In molten state or solution, ions become free to move and carry electric current. This is asked every 2–3 years.
Section 5
Metallurgy & Extraction of Metals
Different metals need different extraction methods — the reactivity of the metal determines the process.
Ore = mineral from which a metal can be profitably extracted. Mineral = any naturally occurring compound of a metal. Gangue = earthy impurities mixed with the ore. Flux = substance added to remove gangue as slag.
💡
Ore vs Mineral — The Key Distinction
All ores are minerals, but NOT all minerals are ores. Bauxite (Al₂O₃·2H₂O) is an ore of aluminium. Clay also contains aluminium compounds, but extraction from clay is not economical — so clay is a mineral, not an ore. This distinction is guaranteed 1 mark.

🔧 Steps in Extraction of Metals

The extraction method depends on the position of the metal in the reactivity series:
ReactivityExamplesMethod
Highly reactive (top)K, Na, Ca, Mg, AlElectrolysis of molten chloride/oxide
Medium reactivity (middle)Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb, CuReduction with coke (carbon) or CO
Low reactivity (bottom)Hg, Ag, Au, PtOccur naturally or obtained by heating alone
1
Concentration of Ore (Beneficiation)
Remove gangue (earthy impurities) from the ore. Methods: Gravity separation, froth flotation (for sulphide ores), magnetic separation (for magnetic ores like Fe₃O₄), leaching (for Al from bauxite).
2
Conversion to Metal Oxide
Oxide ores are directly used. Sulphide ores → Roasting (heated in air). Carbonate ores → Calcination (heated without air).
Roasting: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂↑
Calcination: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂↑
3
Reduction to Metal
For medium-reactivity metals: reduction with coke (C) or CO. Highly reactive metals: electrolysis.
ZnO + C → Zn + CO↑ (carbon reduction)
Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (in blast furnace)
2Al₂O₃ →(electrolysis)→ 4Al + 3O₂ (Hall-Héroult)
4
Refining (Purification)
The crude metal is purified. Most pure method: electrolytic refining (for Cu, Ag, Au, Ni). Other methods: liquation, distillation, zone refining.
⚠️
Roasting vs Calcination — Don't Confuse!
Roasting = heating in excess AIR/O₂. Used for sulphide ores. Produces metal oxide + SO₂.
Calcination = heating without air (or limited air). Used for carbonate/hydroxide ores. Produces metal oxide + CO₂ (or H₂O).
Both convert the ore into the oxide form, which is then reduced. This is a 1-mark question almost every year.

🏭 Blast Furnace (Extraction of Iron)

Iron is extracted from haematite (Fe₂O₃) using a blast furnace. Coke (carbon) is both the fuel and the reducing agent.

Reactions inside the Blast Furnace

Zone 1 (combustion — hottest):
C + O₂ → CO₂

Zone 2 (CO formation):
CO₂ + C → 2CO (carbon monoxide = main reducing agent)

Zone 3 (reduction of iron ore):
Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (iron melts and flows to bottom)

Zone 4 (slag formation — flux removes gangue):
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (flux decomposes)
CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag = calcium silicate, floats on molten iron)
📋
Board Tip — Blast Furnace
The main reducing agent in the blast furnace is CO (carbon monoxide), not C directly. Limestone (CaCO₃) is added as flux. It forms slag (CaSiO₃) which floats on molten iron and is removed. The iron collected is called pig iron — it contains ~4% carbon and is brittle. Pig iron is converted to wrought iron (purest) or steel by removing excess carbon.

⚗️ Extraction of Aluminium (Hall-Héroult Process)

Aluminium is very reactive — it cannot be reduced by carbon. It is extracted by electrolysis of molten alumina (Al₂O₃) dissolved in cryolite.
At cathode (reduction): Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (aluminium metal deposited)
At anode (oxidation): 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻ (O₂ burns carbon anode → CO₂)
💡
Why Cryolite?
Pure Al₂O₃ melts at ~2000°C — too high for practical electrolysis. Cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) is added to lower the melting point to ~950°C and increase electrical conductivity. Fluorspar (CaF₂) is also added. Carbon anodes are gradually consumed by the O₂ produced and must be replaced periodically.

⚡ Electrolytic Refining

The most effective method of purifying metals. Used for copper, silver, gold, nickel.

Why Molten NaCl?
In aqueous NaCl, H₂ forms at cathode (not Na) because H⁺ is preferentially discharged. In molten NaCl, no water → Na⁺ is discharged → Na metal forms at cathode. Cl₂ forms at anode.

Why Al Seems Unreactive
Al is actually reactive, but Al₂O₃ layer is hard, impervious, and sticks firmly. It prevents further corrosion. Iron's rust (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O) is porous and flaky — does NOT protect iron.
Section 6
Corrosion & Prevention
Corrosion costs industries billions every year — understanding it and preventing it is a real-world application of this chapter.
Corrosion = deterioration of a metal due to reaction with its environment (O₂, H₂O, CO₂, acids). Rusting = specific corrosion of iron forming hydrated iron(III) oxide.

Rusting of Iron

4Fe + 3O₂ + xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O
Rust = hydrated iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O). Reddish-brown, porous, flaky. Rusting requires BOTH O₂ AND H₂O — neither alone causes rusting.
❌
Why Rust Does NOT Protect Iron
Rust (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O) is porous and flaky. It keeps falling off, exposing fresh iron to further rusting. Contrast this with Al₂O₃ (aluminium oxide) which is hard, impervious, and sticks firmly — it actually protects aluminium. This distinction is key to HOTS questions.

Methods of Preventing Corrosion

🎨
Painting / Oiling
Barriers prevent contact with air and moisture. Used on iron gates, bridges, machinery.
Limitation: protects only as long as coating is intact. Scratches expose metal.
🔩
Galvanisation
Iron coated with zinc (Zn). Zinc is more reactive — acts as sacrificial anode, corrodes preferentially.
Advantage: protects even when scratched (Zn corrodes, Fe is safe). Used in roofing, buckets.
⚡
Electroplating
Deposit a protective metal layer (Cr, Ni, Sn) by electrolysis. Used on bicycle parts, car bumpers.
Chromium plating = shiny + hard + corrosion-resistant. Tin plating = food cans (tin is non-toxic).
🔬
Alloying
Mixing metals to create alloys with better corrosion resistance. E.g., stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni).
Stainless steel does not rust. Used in kitchen utensils, surgical instruments.
🛡️
Cathodic Protection
Attach a more reactive metal (Mg block) to iron structures. Mg corrodes instead of iron.
Used in pipelines, ship hulls, underground tanks. Also called sacrificial protection.
🫙
Anodising
Electrochemically thicken the Al₂O₃ layer on aluminium — makes the protective layer even stronger.
Used on aluminium cookware, aircraft parts, smartphone frames.
Section 7
Alloys
Pure metals are often too soft, too reactive, or too expensive — alloys solve all these problems.
An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals (or a metal and a non-metal). Alloys have better properties than the pure metals — harder, stronger, lower melting point, more corrosion-resistant, or changed colour.
Brass
Cu (70%) + Zn (30%)
Harder than pure copper. Used in: musical instruments, taps, locks, hinges. Does not spark.
Bronze
Cu + Sn (tin)
Very hard and corrosion-resistant. Used in: statues, coins, bells, ship propellers, medals.
Stainless Steel
Fe + Cr (10–18%) + Ni
Doesn't rust. Used in: kitchen utensils, surgical instruments, automobiles, cutlery.
Solder
Pb + Sn (50:50)
Very low melting point. Used in: soldering electronic circuits, joining metal pieces.
Duralumin
Al + Cu + Mg + Mn
Lightweight and strong. Used in: aircraft bodies, spacecraft, bicycle frames.
Amalgam
Hg + another metal
Mercury alloy. Used in: dental fillings (Hg + Ag + Sn), mirror backing.
German Silver
Cu + Zn + Ni
Silver-coloured, but contains no silver! Used in: jewellery, utensils, electrical contacts.
Rose Gold
Au + Cu
Pink/rose colour. Harder than pure gold. Used in: jewellery, watches.
📋
Why Are Pure Metals Alloyed?
Pure gold (24 carat) is too soft for jewellery — it bends easily. Mixed with Cu/Ag → harder alloy (22 carat = 22 parts Au + 2 parts other metal per 24 parts).
Pure iron is too reactive and brittle → mixed with carbon to make steel (stronger) or with Cr/Ni to make stainless steel (corrosion-resistant).
Pure sodium dissolves explosively in water → not useful as a material by itself.
🧠 Concept Check — Alloys & Corrosion
Q1: An iron nail is dipped in copper sulphate solution. Write the observation and equation.
Observation: The iron nail turns brown/copper-coloured. The blue CuSO₄ solution gradually turns pale green (FeSO₄ forms).
Equation: Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu. Iron (more reactive) displaces copper from its salt solution. This is a displacement reaction.
Q2: Why is galvanisation better than painting for protecting iron?
Painting forms a barrier that protects only as long as the paint is intact. If the paint is scratched, iron is directly exposed to air/moisture and rusts. Galvanisation uses zinc (more reactive than iron). Even if the zinc coating is scratched, zinc corrodes preferentially (sacrificial anode), protecting the iron beneath. Galvanisation provides electrochemical protection, not just a physical barrier.
Q3: German Silver contains no silver. What is its composition and why is it used?
German Silver = Cu + Zn + Ni. It has a silver-like appearance despite containing no silver. It is cheaper than silver, harder, and corrosion-resistant. Used in jewellery, cutlery, and utensils as a silver substitute. It is an example of how alloys can mimic the appearance of expensive metals at lower cost.
