Section 01
Acids
🍋 Lemon is sour. Vinegar makes your face scrunch. Tamarind makes your mouth water. They're all acids — substances with that sharp, tangy edge that comes from a single tiny ion.

An acid is a substance that dissolves in water to produce hydrogen ions (H⁺), also called hydronium ions (H₃O⁺). The H⁺ ion is simply a proton — it doesn't exist alone in solution; it immediately attaches to a water molecule:

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻
💡
Why H₃O⁺ and not H⁺?
A bare proton (H⁺) is just a nucleus — no electrons. It has an astronomically high charge density and instantly bonds to water's lone pair of electrons. That's why acids are always shown producing H₃O⁺ (hydronium ions) in water. Both notations are acceptable in boards, but H₃O⁺ is more accurate.

🔬 Types of Acids

TypeBasisExamples
Natural acidsFound in natureCitric (lemon), acetic (vinegar), lactic (curd), formic (ant sting), tartaric (tamarind)
Mineral acidsDerived from minerals, made in lab/industryHCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃
Strong acidsIonise completely in waterHCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃
Weak acidsIonise partially in waterCH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, H₂SO₃
Dilute acidsSmall amount of acid in large amount of waterVinegar (~5% CH₃COOH)
Concentrated acidsLarge amount of acid in small waterConc. H₂SO₄ (~98%)
⚠️
Strength ≠ Concentration
A common mistake! Strength = degree of ionisation. Concentration = amount of acid per litre. Glacial acetic acid (100% pure CH₃COOH) is highly concentrated but still a weak acid because it ionises very little.

Section 02
Bases & Alkalis
🧼 Soap is slippery. Baking soda fizzes in acid. Antacid tablets cool your burning stomach. Bases — the chemical opposite of acids — are all around you.

A base is a substance that produces hydroxide ions (OH⁻) in water or accepts H⁺ ions from an acid. Bases have a bitter taste and a soapy feel.

An alkali is a base that is soluble in water and produces OH⁻ ions in aqueous solution. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis.

📋
Alkali vs Base — Exam Classic
Cu(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₃, Zn(OH)₂ are bases but NOT alkalis — they are insoluble in water. NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ are alkalis (soluble). The question "Is every base an alkali?" = No. "Is every alkali a base?" = Yes.

🔬 Properties of Bases

😤
Bitter Taste
OH⁻ ions give a bitter, unpleasant taste. Never taste strong bases!
🧴
Soapy Feel
OH⁻ saponifies skin oils — hence the slippery feeling.
🔵
Turn Red Litmus Blue
OH⁻ ions restore litmus to its natural blue state.
⚡
Conduct Electricity
Strong alkalis fully ionise → free Na⁺/K⁺ + OH⁻ carry charge.
🔥
Corrosive (Strong)
NaOH, KOH are caustic — destroy proteins, can cause severe burns.
⚗️
React with Acids
Neutralisation: base + acid → salt + water. Always exothermic.

⚡ Reaction of Bases with Metals (Amphoteric)

Some metals (Zn, Al, Pb, Sn) react with both acids AND strong alkali solutions to produce hydrogen gas. These are called amphoteric metals.

Zinc + NaOH
Zn + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑
Sodium zincate + hydrogen gas (pop sound)
Aluminium + NaOH
2Al + 2NaOH + 2H₂O → 2NaAlO₂ + 3H₂↑
Sodium aluminate + hydrogen gas

Section 03
Indicators & pH
🌈 How do you tell if something is acidic or basic without tasting it? Nature gave us some clever colour-changing chemicals called indicators — and scientists gave us the pH scale.

An indicator is a substance that changes colour in the presence of acids or bases. They allow us to identify the nature of a solution without any dangerous testing.

🌍 pH in Daily Life

🦷
Tooth Decay
Enamel dissolves when pH < 5.5
Bacteria break down sugars → produce acids → pH falls. Below 5.5, hydroxyapatite (enamel) dissolves. Toothpaste (basic) neutralises acids → keeps pH above 5.5.
⭐ Critical pH = 5.5 — memorise this!
🌱
Soil & Plants
Best crop growth: pH 6–7.5
Too acidic → add lime (CaO or Ca(OH)₂) to raise pH. Tea grows in slightly acidic soil (pH 5-6). Most crops prefer slightly acidic to neutral soil.
⭐ Tea = acidic soil. Lime = raises pH.
🫀
Blood & Body
Blood pH = 7.35–7.45
Enzymes work in narrow pH ranges. If blood pH falls below 7.35 → acidosis (life-threatening). Kidneys and lungs regulate blood pH constantly.
⭐ Blood = slightly basic. Not neutral!
🐟
Aquatic Life
Fish survive: pH 7–8.5
Acid rain (pH < 5.6) lowers river/lake pH, killing fish and aquatic organisms. Acid rain caused by SO₂ and NOₓ dissolving in water from industrial burning.
⭐ Acid rain pH < 5.6. Normal rain pH ≈ 5.6.
🐝
Stings & Bites
Bee sting = acidic (formic acid)
Remedy: baking soda solution (base) neutralises acid. Wasp sting = alkaline → remedy: vinegar (acid). Nettle sting = formic acid → same remedy as bee sting.
⭐ Bee/nettle = acid → base remedy. Wasp = base → acid remedy.
🫁
Digestion
Stomach HCl pH = 1–2
HCl activates pepsin for protein digestion. Excess HCl → acidity/heartburn → treat with antacids (Mg(OH)₂, NaHCO₃). Antacids must be mild bases — NOT strong alkalis like NaOH.
⭐ Antacid = mild base. Never NaOH!
🧠 Concept Check — pH
Q1. A solution turns red litmus blue. What is its possible pH range?
Show Answer ▼
pH greater than 7 (i.e., pH 8–14). Red litmus turns blue only in basic/alkaline solutions.
Q2. Two solutions have pH 3 and pH 11. What is the ratio of their H⁺ ion concentrations?
Show Answer ▼
pH 3 → [H⁺] = 10⁻³. pH 11 → [H⁺] = 10⁻¹¹. Ratio = 10⁻³ / 10⁻¹¹ = 10⁸ : 1. The pH 3 solution has 100 million times more H⁺ ions.
Q3. What happens to pH when a base is diluted with water?
Show Answer ▼
pH decreases (moves towards 7). Dilution always moves pH towards 7. A base can never have pH below 7 on diluting — it approaches 7 but never crosses it.
Section 04
Reactions of Acids & Bases
⚗️ Acids and bases are reactive by nature. Learning their reactions is learning the grammar of inorganic chemistry — master these patterns and you'll recognise any reaction.

⚡ Reaction 1: Acid + Metal → Salt + H₂

Most metals above hydrogen in the reactivity series displace hydrogen from dilute acids. Metals below hydrogen (Cu, Ag, Au) do NOT react with dilute acids.

Zinc + Dilute H₂SO₄
Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑
Zinc granules dissolve; H₂ bubbles with pop sound; test tube warms up (exothermic)
Iron + Dilute HCl
Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) → FeCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑
Iron dissolves; pale green FeCl₂ solution forms; H₂ gas evolved
Magnesium + Dilute HCl
Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑
Vigorous reaction; Mg dissolves quickly; H₂ burns with pop
📋
Gas Test — H₂
Bring a burning splint near the gas — it burns with a pop sound. This is the standard test for hydrogen gas. Frequently asked as a 1-mark observation question.

🔥 Reaction 2: Acid + Base → Salt + Water (Neutralisation)

Neutralisation is the reaction between an acid and a base to form a salt and water. It is always exothermic — temperature rises.

HCl + NaOH (typical neutralisation)
HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + Heat
H₂SO₄ + 2KOH
H₂SO₄(aq) + 2KOH(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

The net ionic equation for all neutralisations is the same:

H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) + Heat

💨 Reaction 3: Acid + Carbonate/Bicarbonate → Salt + Water + CO₂

HCl + Sodium Carbonate
Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
Brisk effervescence; CO₂ turns lime water milky
HCl + Sodium Bicarbonate
NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
Lime Water + CO₂ (Two Steps!)
Step 1: Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)↓ + H₂O(l)
Lime water turns MILKY (white CaCO₃ precipitate)
Step 2 (excess CO₂): CaCO₃(s) + CO₂(g) + H₂O(l) → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq)
Milkiness DISAPPEARS — soluble Ca(HCO₃)₂ forms, solution clears
📋
Test for CO₂
Pass gas through lime water — turns milky. Both steps of the lime water reaction are exam favourites. "Milky → clear on excess CO₂" is a very specific observation that scores full marks.

⬛ Reaction 4: Acid + Metal Oxide → Salt + Water

Metal oxides are basic in nature. They react with acids in a neutralisation-type reaction (no gas evolved).

CuO + Dilute HCl
CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
Black CuO dissolves; solution turns BLUE-GREEN (CuCl₂ solution)
ZnO + Dilute H₂SO₄
ZnO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
White ZnO dissolves; colourless solution of ZnSO₄ forms

🔵 Reactions of Bases

Base + Non-metallic Oxide (CO₂)
2NaOH(aq) + CO₂(g) → Na₂CO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)
CO₂ gas absorbed by NaOH
Amphoteric Metal (Zn) + NaOH
Zn(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂(g)↑
H₂ gas evolved; sodium zincate forms in solution

Section 05
Salts
🧂 Table salt, baking soda, bleaching powder, washing soda — all salts. They surround us at home, but their chemistry is deep and fascinating.

A salt is an ionic compound formed by the reaction of an acid and a base. The cation comes from the base and the anion comes from the acid.

🔬 Classification of Salts by pH

TypeFormed FrompHExampleWhy?
Acidic saltStrong acid + Weak base<7NH₄ClNH₄⁺ hydrolyses → H⁺ released
Basic saltWeak acid + Strong base>7Na₂CO₃, CH₃COONaCO₃²⁻ hydrolyses → OH⁻ released
Neutral saltStrong acid + Strong base=7NaCl, K₂SO₄Neither ion hydrolyses
📋
Salt Nature — The Rule
SA + SB = Neutral (7) | SA + WB = Acidic (<7) | WA + SB = Basic (>7)
SA = Strong acid, SB = Strong base, WA = Weak acid, WB = Weak base. This rule generates most of the salt pH questions in boards.

⭐ The 5 Important Salts

Common Salt
NaCl
Sodium chloride. The most abundant salt. Found in sea water and rock salt deposits. Used as a raw material (brine) for chlor-alkali process. Neutral salt (pH 7).
Uses: food preservation, raw material for NaOH/Cl₂/HCl, de-icing roads
Caustic Soda
NaOH
Sodium hydroxide. Strong, corrosive alkali. Highly soluble in water — process is exothermic. Deliquescent (absorbs moisture from air). Reacts with CO₂ from air.
Uses: making soap, paper, rayon, degreasing metals, de-blocking drains
Baking Soda
NaHCO₃
Sodium hydrogen carbonate (sodium bicarbonate). Mild basic salt (pH ≈ 8.3). Decomposes on heating: 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑. The CO₂ makes bread rise.
Uses: baking (leavening agent), antacid, fire extinguishers (soda-acid type)
Washing Soda
Na₂CO₃·10H₂O
Sodium carbonate decahydrate. Basic salt (pH ~11). Efflorescent — loses water of crystallisation in dry air → white powder. Made from baking soda (heat NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃, then crystallise).
Uses: remove water hardness, glass making, soap/paper/detergent industry
Bleaching Powder
CaOCl₂
Calcium oxychloride. Mixed salt — contains both Cl⁻ and OCl⁻ ions. Made by passing Cl₂ over dry slaked lime Ca(OH)₂: Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O at ~313K. Pungent chlorine smell.
Uses: bleaching cotton/linen/paper, disinfecting water, oxidising agent
Plaster of Paris
CaSO₄·½H₂O
Calcium sulphate hemihydrate. Made by heating gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) at 373K: CaSO₄·2H₂O → CaSO₄·½H₂O + 3/2H₂O. Sets by absorbing water back → reverts to gypsum. Slight expansion on setting.
Uses: surgical casts, dental moulds, ceilings, decorative work
⚠️
Dead Burnt Plaster
If gypsum is heated above 120°C (373K), all water is lost → anhydrous CaSO₄ (dead burnt plaster) forms. It cannot set with water — the crystallisation sites are permanently destroyed. Temperature control in POP preparation is critical.
🧠 Concept Check — Salts
Q1. Washing soda crystals left in open air turn to white powder. Name this process and explain why.
Show Answer ▼
Efflorescence. Na₂CO₃·10H₂O loses its 10 water molecules (water of crystallisation) to dry air, turning into anhydrous white Na₂CO₃ powder. The crystals lose their shape and lustre.
Q2. Why is baking soda used in fire extinguishers?
Show Answer ▼
When NaHCO₃ reacts with acid (in soda-acid extinguisher) or is heated, it produces CO₂: NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑. The CO₂ gas smothers the fire by cutting off its oxygen supply. CO₂ is a non-combustible gas.
Q3. How is washing soda made from baking soda? Give equations.
Show Answer ▼
Step 1: Heat baking soda → 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑
Step 2: Dissolve Na₂CO₃ in water and crystallise → Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O (washing soda)
Section 06
Chlor-Alkali Process
⚡ Salt + electricity = three industrial giants in one reaction. The chlor-alkali process is one of the most important industrial chemical processes in the world.

The chlor-alkali process is the electrolysis of brine (concentrated aqueous NaCl solution). It produces three commercially important products in a single process.

Balanced Equation
2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) →(electricity) 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g)↑ + H₂(g)↑
🔵 CATHODE (−)
H₂ ↑
Hydrogen gas bubbles up at negative electrode

Reduction: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂
Brine
NaCl(aq)
NaOH forms here
⚡
🔴 ANODE (+)
Cl₂ ↑
Chlorine gas produced at positive electrode

Oxidation: 2Cl⁻ − 2e⁻ → Cl₂
📋
Why "Chlor-Alkali"?
"Chlor" = chlorine (Cl₂ produced). "Alkali" = NaOH (sodium hydroxide, a strong alkali) produced. The name literally tells you the two main products. H₂ is the bonus third product!

🏭 Uses of the Three Products

ProductUses
Cl₂ (Chlorine)Bleaching cotton/linen/paper, manufacturing PVC and solvents, manufacturing HCl, disinfecting drinking water, manufacturing chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)
H₂ (Hydrogen)Fuel for fuel cells (clean energy), manufacturing margarine (hydrogenation), in the Haber process (making fertilisers), as a rocket fuel
NaOH (Caustic Soda)Manufacturing soaps and detergents, making paper and rayon (textiles), degreasing metals, manufacturing artificial fibres
⚠️
Why Electrodes Must Be Separated
If Cl₂ and NaOH meet in solution, they react to form sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl): Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O. This contaminates the NaOH product. Also, Cl₂ + H₂ mixture is explosive. The electrolytic cell uses a membrane to separate anode and cathode compartments.
Section 07
Water of Crystallisation
💎 Why does copper sulphate turn white when heated? Why does washing soda turn powdery when left in air? The answer is hidden inside the crystal — as water molecules locked in structure.

Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a crystalline salt. These water molecules are an integral part of the crystal lattice and are responsible for the characteristic shape and often colour of the salt.

💡
How to Identify Water of Crystallisation
The number after the dot (·) in the formula. Na₂CO₃·10H₂O has 10 water molecules. CaSO₄·½H₂O has half a water molecule per formula unit. When heated, these salts lose the water → colour/shape changes.

🔬 The Blue Vitriol Experiment

This classic NCERT activity demonstrates water of crystallisation beautifully:

Heating CuSO₄·5H₂O (Blue → White)
CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) →(heat) CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(g)
Blue crystals → white powder. Water vapour condenses on cooler parts of the tube.
Adding Water to Anhydrous CuSO₄ (White → Blue)
CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(l) → CuSO₄·5H₂O(s)
White powder → blue again! Reaction is reversible and exothermic.
📋
Test for Water Using CuSO₄
White anhydrous CuSO₄ turns blue in the presence of water. This is used as a chemical test for water. Very common 1-mark question: "Anhydrous CuSO₄ is used to test for ___ . It turns ___ colour."

🧠 Concept Check — Water of Crystallisation
Q1. A student heats blue copper sulphate crystals and observes them turning white. Later, he adds a drop of water to the white powder and the blue colour returns. Explain both changes.
Show Answer ▼
Heating removes the 5 water molecules of crystallisation: CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ + 5H₂O. Loss of water destroys the crystal structure that gives the blue colour → white anhydrous CuSO₄.
Adding water: CuSO₄ + 5H₂O → CuSO₄·5H₂O. Water molecules re-enter the crystal lattice, restoring the blue colour. This also shows the reaction is reversible.
Q2. Why does gypsum have 2 waters but POP has only ½ water?
Show Answer ▼
Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) is heated at 373K (100°C) → it loses 3/2 of its water molecules, leaving behind POP (CaSO₄·½H₂O). So POP is gypsum that has had most of its water removed by controlled heating. Above 120°C, all water is lost → dead burnt plaster (CaSO₄) — which can no longer set.

📖 Key Definitions
Acid: Produces H⁺ (H₃O⁺) in water. pH < 7.
Base: Produces OH⁻ in water. pH > 7.
Alkali: A base soluble in water. All alkalis = bases. Not all bases = alkalis.
Neutralisation: Acid + Base → Salt + Water. Always exothermic.
Salt: Ionic compound from acid + base. Cation from base, anion from acid.
pH: −log[H⁺]. Scale 0–14. 0 = max acid, 7 = neutral, 14 = max base.
Water of crystallisation: Fixed H₂O molecules in a crystal's formula unit.
Efflorescence: Loss of water of crystallisation to dry air → white powder.
Deliquescence: Absorption of moisture from air → becomes wet/liquid.
Olfactory indicator: Smell changes in acid/base (onion, vanilla).

🌍 pH Values to Remember
Gastric juice (HCl): pH 1–2
Tooth decay threshold: pH < 5.5
Normal rain: pH ≈ 5.6
Acid rain: pH < 5.6
Blood: pH 7.35–7.45
Sea water: pH ≈ 8.4
Soap solution: pH ~9–10
Baking soda: pH ≈ 8.3
NaOH (1M): pH ≈ 13–14
🚫 Common Exam Mistakes
Dry HCl does NOT change dry litmus. Water is essential.
Strength ≠ Concentration. 10M CH₃COOH is still weak.
All alkalis are bases, but NOT all bases are alkalis (Cu(OH)₂ is not an alkali).
Antacids must be mild bases — never NaOH (too corrosive).
Add ACID to WATER (AAW), never water to concentrated acid.
Chlor-alkali: Cl₂ at ANODE, H₂ at CATHODE, NaOH in solution.
POP heated above 120°C → dead burnt plaster (cannot set).
Bee sting = acidic → remedy is BASE. Wasp = alkaline → ACID.
Diluting acid → pH increases (towards 7). Never exceeds 7.
Blood pH = 7.35–7.45. NOT neutral. Slightly basic.
🎯 Indicator Colours
Litmus: Acid=Red, Base=Blue, Neutral=Purple
Phenolphthalein: Acid=Colourless, Base=Pink
Methyl orange: Acid=Red, Base=Yellow
Turmeric: Acid=Yellow, Base=Red-brown
Universal indicator (by pH):
1-3=Red, 4-5=Orange, 6=Yellow, 7=Green, 8-9=Blue, 10-14=Violet
Olfactory (onion/vanilla): Smell disappears in BASE
⚡ Salt Classification Rule
SA + SB = Neutral (pH 7) → NaCl, K₂SO₄
SA + WB = Acidic (pH < 7) → NH₄Cl, NH₄NO₃
WA + SB = Basic (pH > 7) → Na₂CO₃, CH₃COONa
SA=HCl,H₂SO₄,HNO₃ | WA=CH₃COOH,H₂CO₃
SB=NaOH,KOH,Ca(OH)₂ | WB=NH₄OH,Mg(OH)₂
Neutralisation: H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O + Heat (net ionic)
