Section 1
Carbon — The Wonder Element
Carbon forms more compounds than all other elements combined. Two simple properties explain why.

Carbon (atomic number 6) has the electronic configuration 2,4 — giving it 4 valence electrons. It neither loses all 4 (too much energy) nor gains 4 to complete the shell (too much electron repulsion). Instead, carbon shares electrons — forming covalent bonds exclusively. This single fact unlocks millions of compounds.

Why Carbon Forms So Many Compounds

Two unique properties explain carbon's extraordinary chemistry:

1. Catenation

Carbon atoms can bond with each other, forming long chains, branched chains, and rings. No other element shows this to the same extent — Silicon (Si) can catenate slightly, but Si–Si bonds are far weaker than C–C bonds.

C–C–C–C (chain) | C(C)–C–C (branch) | ⬡ (ring)
2. Tetravalency

Carbon has 4 valence electrons and can form 4 bonds simultaneously — with carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, halogens, sulphur, and more. It can also form double bonds (C=C) and triple bonds (C≡C), multiplying variety further.

C: 4 bonds → C–H, C–O, C=C, C≡N all possible
📋
Board Must-Know
The answer to "Why does carbon form so many compounds?" must use both words: Catenation and Tetravalency. Writing only one gets half marks. Catenation = C–C bonding (chains, rings, branches). Tetravalency = 4 bonds possible with many different elements.

Allotropes of Carbon

Allotropes are different structural forms of the same element. Carbon has three main allotropes — with radically different properties despite being pure carbon.

💎
Diamond
Each C bonded to 4 others (tetrahedral, 3D network)
  • Hardest natural substance
  • Very high melting point (~3550°C)
  • Does NOT conduct electricity (no free e⁻)
  • Transparent, brilliant lustre
  • Used in: cutting tools, jewellery, drill bits
  • Bond angle: 109.5°
🖤
Graphite
Each C bonded to 3 others (hexagonal layers)
  • Soft, slippery (layers slide easily)
  • Good conductor of electricity (free e⁻ between layers)
  • Dark grey, metallic lustre
  • Used in: pencil lead, lubricant, electrodes
  • 4th electron = delocalised (free to move)
  • Only non-metal conductor (exception!)
⚽
Buckminsterfullerene
60 C atoms in a football/cage shape (C₆₀)
  • Spherical molecule — looks like a football
  • 20 hexagons + 12 pentagons
  • Used in nanotechnology, drug delivery
  • Also called "Buckyballs"
  • Discovered 1985 (Nobel Prize 1996)
  • Can trap other atoms inside (endofullerenes)
💡
Diamond vs Graphite — Why Such Different Properties?
Both are pure carbon. Diamond: every electron in C–C bonds → no free electrons → insulator. Graphite: each C uses only 3 bonds (leaves 1 e⁻ free per atom) → free electrons move between layers → conductor. Graphite layers held by weak forces → slides easily → lubricant. Diamond's 3D network → rigid → hardest.
🧠 Concept Check — Allotropes
Q1. Why is graphite used as a lubricant but diamond is not?
Graphite has a layered structure with weak forces between layers, so layers slide over each other easily → acts as lubricant. Diamond has a rigid 3D tetrahedral network where all carbon atoms are strongly bonded in all directions → cannot slide → not a lubricant.
Q2. Both diamond and graphite are forms of carbon. Yet diamond is an insulator and graphite is a conductor. Explain.
In diamond, each carbon forms 4 bonds, using all 4 valence electrons. No free electrons → cannot conduct electricity. In graphite, each carbon forms only 3 bonds, leaving one electron per carbon delocalised (free) between layers. These free electrons carry charge → graphite conducts electricity.
Section 2
Hydrocarbons & Homologous Series
Compounds with only carbon and hydrogen. The pattern of their series is the key to predicting properties.

A hydrocarbon is a compound containing only carbon and hydrogen. They are the simplest organic compounds. Hydrocarbons are classified as saturated (only single bonds — alkanes) or unsaturated (double or triple bonds — alkenes, alkynes).

A homologous series is a group of organic compounds that: (1) share the same general formula, (2) differ from each other by a –CH₂– unit (14 mass units), (3) have the same functional group, and (4) show similar chemical properties with a gradual change in physical properties.

📋
4 Features of Homologous Series — Must Write All 4
(1) Same general formula  (2) Consecutive members differ by –CH₂– (14 u)  (3) Same functional group  (4) Similar chemical properties; physical properties change gradually. Missing any one feature loses marks.

Saturated vs Unsaturated — The Key Test
Add bromine water (orange/brown) to the compound. Unsaturated (alkene/alkyne): bromine water decolourises (colour disappears) → Br₂ adds across the double/triple bond. Saturated (alkane): no decolourisation. This is the standard practical test for unsaturation in boards.

⚠️
Common IUPAC Mistake
CH₃COOH — students often say "2 carbons = ethanoic acid" correctly, but CH₃CH₂COOH has 3 carbons → propanoic acid. Always count all carbons including the one in –COOH. The –COOH carbon is carbon-1 by convention.

Isomers — Same Formula, Different Structure

Isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural formulae. They have different physical properties (boiling points, melting points) but sometimes similar chemical properties if the functional group is the same.

Section 4
Chemical Reactions of Carbon Compounds
Four reaction types dominate this chapter. Know each one's conditions and products cold.

1. Combustion

All carbon compounds burn in oxygen to produce carbon dioxide and water. This is combustion. The completeness depends on oxygen supply.

🔥 Complete Combustion
Excess oxygen → CO₂ + H₂O (clean blue flame)
Methane (Natural Gas / CNG)
CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat
Ethanol
C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O + heat
✅ Blue flame · No soot · Products: CO₂ + H₂O only
💨 Incomplete Combustion
Limited oxygen → CO (toxic) and/or soot
Limited O₂
CH₄ + 1½O₂ → CO + 2H₂O  (or)  CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O
⚠️ Yellow/sooty flame · CO is odourless and poisonous · Carbon (soot) deposits
📋
Board Tip: Why Fuels with Long Chains Have Yellow Flames
Long-chain hydrocarbons (kerosene, petrol) don't burn cleanly because they need more oxygen per molecule. Without sufficient O₂, incomplete combustion gives a yellow/sooty flame with CO and soot. LPG burns with a blue flame because it's a short-chain (propane/butane) compound requiring less O₂.

2. Addition Reaction (Unsaturated compounds only)

Unsaturated compounds (alkenes, alkynes) undergo addition reactions. Two reactant molecules combine to form one product. The double or triple bond is broken and new atoms add across it.

⚗️ Hydrogenation (H₂ addition)
Oil → Vanaspati Ghee
Vegetable Oil + Hydrogen (Ni catalyst, 150°C)
CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ →(Ni, 150°C)→ CH₃–CH₃
✅ Unsaturated oil (liquid) → saturated fat (solid vanaspati). Catalyst: Nickel (Ni). Industrial scale.
⚗️ Addition of Bromine (Br₂)
Test for Unsaturation
Ethene + Bromine water
CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₂Br–CH₂Br  (1,2-dibromoethane)
✅ Orange bromine water → colourless. This decolourisation = test for C=C double bond.

3. Substitution Reaction (Saturated compounds only)

Saturated hydrocarbons (alkanes) cannot undergo addition. Instead, they undergo substitution — one atom (usually H) is replaced by another (usually a halogen). Requires sunlight to initiate.

☀️ Chlorination of Methane
H replaced by Cl (in sunlight)
Step 1 — monosubstitution
CH₄ + Cl₂ →(sunlight)→ CH₃Cl + HCl  (chloromethane)
Step 2 — further substitution possible
CH₃Cl → CH₂Cl₂ → CHCl₃ → CCl₄  (chloroform → carbon tetrachloride)
⚠️ Sunlight is essential. This is a free-radical chain reaction.
📋
Addition vs Substitution — The Classic Distinction
Unsaturated (alkene/alkyne) → Addition reaction (Br₂ adds across double bond).
Saturated (alkane) → Substitution reaction (H replaced by Cl/Br, needs sunlight).
This is a direct 1-mark question every year: "What type of reaction does ethene undergo with Br₂?" → Addition.

4. Oxidation Reactions

Carbon compounds can be oxidised using alkaline KMnO₄ or acidified K₂Cr₂O₇. Alcohols are oxidised to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids. This is also the basis of the breathalyser test.

🔬 Oxidation of Ethanol
Alcohol → Acid (KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇)
C₂H₅OH →(alk. KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇)→ CH₃COOH
✅ Ethanol (alcohol) oxidised to ethanoic acid. Same reaction happens naturally when wine is exposed to air (bacteria oxidise it → vinegar).
🌿 Esterification (Condensation)
Alcohol + Acid → Ester + Water
Ethanol + Ethanoic acid (conc. H₂SO₄, heat)
CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O
✅ Ethyl ethanoate (fruity smell, used in perfumes, nail polish remover). Reaction is REVERSIBLE (⇌). H₂SO₄ = catalyst + dehydrating agent.
🧼 Saponification
Ester + NaOH → Soap (sodium salt) + Glycerol
Hydrolysis of fat/oil with NaOH
Fat (triglyceride) + 3NaOH → Soap (3 sodium fatty acid salts) + Glycerol
✅ NaOH → hard soap. KOH → soft/liquid soap. This is BASE HYDROLYSIS of an ester. NOT reversible (unlike esterification).

Section 5
Ethanol (C₂H₅OH)
The most important alcohol in the syllabus — and the most tested. Know all its reactions cold.

Ethanol (common name: ethyl alcohol) is a colourless liquid with a characteristic smell. Molecular formula: C₂H₅OH. It is the alcohol present in alcoholic beverages (produced by fermentation of sugars/starch).

PropertyValue / Details
IUPAC NameEthanol
Molecular FormulaC₂H₅OH (or C₂H₆O)
Boiling Point78.4°C (higher than ethane due to H-bonding via –OH)
SolubilityCompletely miscible with water (–OH group)
State at room tempLiquid
SmellPleasant, characteristic

Key Reactions of Ethanol

⚗️ With Sodium Metal
Ethanol + Na → Hydrogen gas
2C₂H₅OH + 2Na → 2C₂H₅ONa + H₂↑
✅ Sodium ethoxide formed. H₂ gives "pop" with burning splint. Proves –OH group has weak acidic character.
🌡️ Dehydration (Elimination)
Ethanol → Ethene (remove H₂O)
C₂H₅OH →(conc. H₂SO₄, 170°C)→ CH₂=CH₂ + H₂O
⚠️ Excess H₂SO₄ + high temp (170°C) = ethene. At lower temp (140°C) with less H₂SO₄ = diethyl ether. Temperature determines product!
🔬 Oxidation
Ethanol → Ethanoic Acid
C₂H₅OH →(alk. KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇)→ CH₃COOH
✅ KMnO₄ (purple) decolourises. Also happens naturally: wine + bacteria + O₂ → vinegar (5% ethanoic acid).
🔥 Combustion
Burns with blue flame
C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O + heat  (complete combustion)
✅ Blue flame. Ethanol is used as a fuel (spirit lamps) and is blended with petrol.
❌
Methanol vs Ethanol — Critical Safety Difference
Ethanol (C₂H₅OH): present in alcoholic beverages. Safe in moderate quantities.
Methanol (CH₃OH): EXTREMELY TOXIC. Even 10 mL can cause permanent blindness; 30 mL can be fatal. It is converted to formaldehyde in the body. This is why illicit liquor (hooch) made with methanol causes mass casualties. Always in boards.
💡
Fermentation — How Ethanol is Made
C₆H₁₂O₆ →(yeast, anaerobic)→ 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂
Glucose is converted to ethanol + CO₂ by yeast enzymes (zymase) in the absence of oxygen (anaerobic). Temperature must be 25–30°C. Above 40°C, yeast dies. Below 15°C, reaction is too slow.
Section 6
Ethanoic Acid (CH₃COOH)
The acid in vinegar — and one of the most tested compounds in this chapter.

Ethanoic acid (common name: acetic acid) is a carboxylic acid. Molecular formula: CH₃COOH. Dilute solution (~5%) is vinegar. Pure ethanoic acid is called glacial acetic acid — it freezes at 16.6°C (just below room temperature) forming ice-like crystals.

PropertyValue / Details
IUPAC NameEthanoic acid
Molecular FormulaCH₃COOH (or C₂H₄O₂)
Boiling Point118°C (very high — strong H-bonding + dimerisation)
Melting Point16.6°C (solid at winter temps → "glacial acetic acid")
SmellPungent, vinegar-like
pH< 7 (weak acid)

Key Reactions of Ethanoic Acid

🧪 With NaHCO₃ (Test for –COOH)
CO₂ gas with fizzing — diagnostic test
CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑
✅ Brisk effervescence. CO₂ turns lime water milky. Ethanol does NOT react with NaHCO₃ (not acidic enough). This is the key test to distinguish acid from alcohol.
🧪 With Na₂CO₃
Also gives CO₂
2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑
🌸 Esterification
With ethanol → fruity ester
CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O  (conc. H₂SO₄, heat)
✅ Ethyl ethanoate — fruity smell. Reversible. Conc. H₂SO₄ = catalyst. Used in perfumes, nail polish remover, artificial fruit flavours.
🧼 With NaOH (Neutralisation)
Acid + Base → Salt + Water
CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O
✅ Sodium ethanoate (sodium acetate) formed. Solution turns basic (pH > 7) — sodium ethanoate is a salt of a weak acid and strong base.
🧠 Concept Check — Ethanol vs Ethanoic Acid
Q. Both ethanol and ethanoic acid contain –OH. Why does only ethanoic acid react with NaHCO₃?
Ethanoic acid has a carboxyl group (–COOH) which is strongly acidic — it can donate H⁺ ions readily enough to react with NaHCO₃ (a weak base) to give CO₂. Ethanol has a hydroxyl group (–OH) which is only very weakly acidic — it cannot donate H⁺ strongly enough to react with NaHCO₃. This distinguishes alcohols from carboxylic acids.
Q. What is glacial acetic acid? Why is it called "glacial"?
Glacial acetic acid is pure, undiluted ethanoic acid (100% concentration). It is called "glacial" because it freezes at 16.6°C — just below room temperature in cold weather — forming ice-like (glacial) crystals. Vinegar is only ~5% ethanoic acid in water.
Section 7
Soaps & Detergents
How a molecule with two different ends can remove dirt from both water and oil.

Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids. They are made by saponification — alkaline hydrolysis of fats/oils with NaOH or KOH.

🧼 Structure of Soap Molecule
HYDROPHILIC HEAD (–COO⁻Na⁺)
Ionic, polar, water-loving end. Attracted to water molecules.
⟷
HYDROPHOBIC TAIL (–CH₂–CH₂– chain)
Non-polar, water-hating, oil-loving end. Repels water, attracted to grease/dirt.

How Soap Cleans — Micelle Formation

When soap is added to water with greasy dirt:

1
The hydrophobic tail buries itself into the grease/oil droplet (like with like).
2
The hydrophilic head points outward into the water.
3
A micelle forms — a spherical cluster with grease trapped inside. The charged outer surface keeps micelles suspended in water (emulsification).
4
Rinsing with water washes the micelles (+ enclosed grease/dirt) away.

📋
Why Soaps Don't Work in Hard Water
Hard water contains Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions. These react with soap (sodium stearate): 2C₁₇H₃₅COONa + CaCl₂ → (C₁₇H₃₅COO)₂Ca↓ + 2NaCl. The calcium/magnesium soap is insoluble → forms a grey scum. No micelles form → no cleaning. Detergents have branched hydrophobic chains that don't react with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺.
🧠 Concept Check — Soaps & Detergents
Q. What is a micelle? Why is it important in the cleaning action of soap?
A micelle is a spherical cluster formed by soap molecules in water, where the hydrophobic tails are directed inward (surrounding and trapping grease/oil) and the hydrophilic ionic heads face outward into the water. The charged outer surface gives the micelle a negative charge — causing micelles to repel each other and remain suspended in water as a stable emulsion. When rinsed, the micelles carry the grease away with them — this is how soap cleans.
Q. A student used soap to wash clothes but found it didn't lather well and left a grey scum. Explain.
The student used hard water, which contains dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions. These react with soap molecules to form insoluble calcium/magnesium salts (scum). The soap is "used up" reacting with these ions instead of cleaning the clothes. Since no micelles form, the cleaning action is poor. Detergents should be used in hard water instead, as they don't react with Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺.
